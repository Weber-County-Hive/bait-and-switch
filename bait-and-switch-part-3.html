CASE 9 GRADE D

H.B. 475 (2026), "Economic Development Council" — The Oversight Work Group That Vanished

Chief Sponsor: Rep. Calvin Roberts · Senate Sponsor: Sen. Kirk A. Cullimore

The bill created a new economic-development work group with named oversight seats built in. A floor substitute adopted roughly two minutes before final House passage quietly stripped those oversight seats out, without floor debate addressing the change on the record.

Verdict

✓Confirmed against the bill's own substitute history: the oversight seats present in the earlier version are absent from the substitute adopted immediately before passage.
?Who requested the substitute, and why, isn't addressed in the public floor record.
Full case file — Bill-Tracker
This case has its own deep-dive page with the complete timeline and sourcing: "The Oversight Work Group That Vanished Two Minutes Before Passage," CASE 2026-475-BSW.

Added Oct. 18, 2026 (record check): The bill's own title was "Development Planning and Coordination Amendments" in all five versions (introduced, three substitutes and enrolled), per the version texts on le.utah.gov. The names in this case's heading are the names of the council the bill creates. The introduced text "establishes the Economic Opportunity Coordinating Council" and "requires the council to create a work group to provide oversight for the opportunity zone application process in 2026." The 2nd substitute no longer contains a work group. The bill's status page shows the House adopted that substitute at 4:02 p.m. on Feb. 20, 2026 and passed the bill 61–4–10 at 4:04 p.m. The council's name became "Economic Development Council" in the 3rd substitute, which the Senate adopted on the floor March 4, 2026. Signed March 24, 2026. This check confirmed a work group "to provide oversight"; it did not confirm named seats.

CASE 10 GRADE C-

H.B. 492 (2026), "Transportation, Infrastructure, and Housing Amendments" — The Loan That Lost Its Label

Chief Sponsor: Rep. Calvin Roberts · Senate Sponsor: Sen. Kirk A. Cullimore · final substitute sponsored by Rep. Ken Ivory

As introduced, an $18 million loan inside this bill's new housing-infrastructure fund was explicitly labeled money for prison construction, with its own dedicated Utah Code section. The same introduced bill proposed merging two overlapping housing boards into one. By the 1st substitute, three weeks later, the prison label and the merger were both gone — the loan survives unlabeled inside a fund now branded for housing, and the state ended up with two boards instead of one.

Verdict

✓Confirmed directly against the bill's own tracked-changes redlines: both the prison-infrastructure language and the board-repeal language are present in the introduced text and absent from the 1st substitute (Feb. 18, 2026, committee vote 8-0-2).
✓The state's own official fiscal note repeats the same omission — it describes the $18M loan only by recipient, never by original purpose.
?Who specifically moved the Feb. 18 substitute, and whether either change was discussed on the record, isn't yet confirmed — only the aggregate 8-0-2 committee tally is.
Full case file — Bill-Tracker
This case has its own deep-dive page with the complete timeline and sourcing: "The Loan That Lost Its Label," CASE 2026-492-BSW.

Added Oct. 18, 2026 (record check): The bill's status page confirms the House committee substitute on Feb. 18, 2026 (8–0–2), House passage 61–8–6 on Feb. 24, a Senate floor substitute at 9:38 p.m. on March 6, a conference committee substitute at 11:28 p.m. that night, and the governor's signature on March 25, 2026. The bill's title stayed the same in all seven versions.

CASE 11 OF 19

The Cap That Got an Exemption, and the Fix That Never Got a Vote

S.B. 188 (2025)H.B. 332 (2026)

S.B. 188: Sen. Keith Grover (primary) / Rep. Stephanie Gricius  ·  H.B. 332: Rep. Tiara Auxier (primary)

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKES.B. 188, "School District Modifications," was a bill about splitting school districts: elections for new boards, start-up money and how debt is shared. Its title never changed in six versions.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSIts final version says that when voters in a dividing district rejected a general obligation bond in the past three years, but voters in the area of a new district favored it, the old board "shall issue a lease revenue bond" for that new district, and that bond "is not subject to the combined total limitation described in Subsection 11-14-103(6)(b)(i)." A 2026 bill to require a public vote before any school lease revenue bond was held in committee and never reached the floor.

The exemption is in the enrolled text of S.B. 188. The Legislature wrote its reason into the bill: the bond is exempt "due to the prior approval of voters within the new or reorganized new school district for the general obligation bond." That is a real argument, and it is quoted here in full. A lease revenue bond does not go to voters at all.

Earlier Hive research found the exemption was added in the House Education Committee on Feb. 27, 2025, in Amendment #2 moved by Rep. Matt MacPherson. The status page shows that committee's amendment votes were recorded, 12–0–4. The bill then went through a conference committee on the last day, 3:19 p.m., and passed both chambers without a "no" vote.

In 2026, H.B. 332 was introduced as "Local Building Authority Amendments" and renamed "School District Project Financing Amendments" in its 1st substitute (le.utah.gov). It would have required a school district to put a general obligation bond to voters before issuing a lease revenue bond. On Feb. 13, 2026 the House Political Subdivisions Committee approved a substitute 12–0–0 and then voted 12–0–0 to hold the bill. It was returned to the Rules Committee on March 2 and closed out at 11:59 p.m. on March 6 with 470 other bills (see Part 4).

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • 2024Earlier Hive research identified the combined cap as $200 million over three years, set by S.B. 86 (2024). This check did not re-open S.B. 86.
  • Feb. 4, 2025Senate Education Committee substitute, 5–0–2.
  • Feb. 27, 2025House Education Committee: substitute and two amendments, each 12–0–4. Earlier Hive research places the cap exemption in Amendment #2.
  • March 7, 2025Conference committee substitute at 3:19 p.m.; House 68–0–7 at 4:43 p.m.; Senate 24–0–5 at 5:52 p.m.
  • March 17, 2025Signed by the governor.
  • Feb. 13, 2026H.B. 332: committee substitute 12–0–0, then held 12–0–0.
  • March 6, 2026, 11:59 p.m.H.B. 332 closed out without a floor vote.
D
Power: A cap meant to limit borrowing without a public vote now has a written exemption, and the bill that would have required a vote first never reached either floor.
Transparency: The exemption and its reason are in plain text in the enrolled law, and every committee and floor vote was recorded. That keeps this from an F.

Open: Earlier Hive research found Alpine School District later brought about $201 million in lease revenue bonds to market. That figure has not been checked against the bond documents, so it is not counted here.

CASE 12 OF 19

The One That Went the Other Way

H.B. 530 (2025)

Sponsor: Rep. Karen M. Peterson (primary) / Sen. Kirk A. Cullimore

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEH.B. 530, "Utah Innovation Lab Modifications," moved the Utah innovation fund under the Nucleus Institute and set rules for the fund, including its yearly audit.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSThe audit language changed twice before passage, and the final version is stronger for the public than the first draft.

As introduced, the yearly audit could be done by the state auditor or by an independent auditor "selected by the institute board, chosen from a list of auditors approved by the state auditor." The 1st substitute changed that to an independent auditor engaged by the state auditor, "selected in consultation with the executive director." A House committee amendment on Feb. 25, 2025 (7–0–3) replaced it with "the state auditor's designee," and added that the designee may not have a business, contractual or other relationship with the institute (enrolled text; status page).

This case is here for balance. A bill can change between drafts in the public's favor, and this one did, on a recorded vote.

Our earlier notes called this bill the "Nucleus Institute Act." Its title on le.utah.gov is "Utah Innovation Lab Modifications."

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Feb. 24, 20251st substitute filed by the sponsor in House Rules.
  • Feb. 25, 2025House committee amendment, 7–0–3: the audit goes to the state auditor's designee.
  • Feb. 27, 2025House passed it 71–0–4.
  • March 4, 2025Senate passed it 26–0–3.
  • March 27, 2025Signed by the governor.
B
Power: The audited group no longer picks its own auditor.
Transparency: The fix came by a recorded committee vote. It took two rounds to get there, which is why this is a B and not an A.
CASE 13 OF 19

A Raise for One Office Became a Raise for Another

S.B. 235 (2026)

Sponsor: Sen. Brady Brammer (primary) / Rep. Jordan D. Teuscher

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEIntroduced Feb. 2, 2026 as "Attorney General Compensation Amendments." It set the attorney general's salary equal to the chief justice of the Utah Supreme Court's total pay, starting July 1, 2026. The Senate passed that version 25–0–4.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSIn the House, a committee substitute renamed it "Governor Compensation Amendments." The law now ties the governor's salary to the chief justice's total pay. The attorney general stays at 95% of the governor's salary, as before.

The change came in the House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee on March 4, 2026 (10–0–6), after the bill was lifted from a different committee that had sent it back to Rules. The House passed the new version 67–2–6 the same afternoon, and the Senate agreed to it the next morning, 26–2–1 (status page; introduced text; enrolled text).

Because the attorney general, lieutenant governor, state auditor and state treasurer are paid a share of the governor's salary (95% or 90%), a higher governor's salary raises theirs too. The attorney general still ends up with more, just by a different route.

Both chambers voted on the final version, and every vote was recorded. What changed is who the bill was about, and that happened in the second chamber, with two days left in the session.

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Feb. 2, 2026Introduced as "Attorney General Compensation Amendments."
  • Feb. 24, 2026Senate passed the attorney general version 25–0–4.
  • March 2, 2026House Government Operations Committee returned it to Rules.
  • March 4, 2026, 2:11 p.m.House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee substitute, 10–0–6: renamed "Governor Compensation Amendments."
  • March 4, 2026, 5:49 p.m.House passed it 67–2–6.
  • March 5, 2026Senate concurred 26–2–1.
  • March 18, 2026Signed by the governor.
C
Power: No public check was removed; the pay formula moved from one office to another.
Transparency: The new title says plainly what the bill does, and both chambers voted on it. The subject changed late, in the second chamber.

Correction to our earlier notes (Oct. 18, 2026): Earlier research notes said Sen. Michael McKell introduced this bill and Sen. Brammer took it over. The introduced text lists Sen. Brady Brammer as primary sponsor from the start. Those notes also said the 1st substitute kept the attorney general provision; the substitute's summary covers only the governor.

CASE 14 OF 19

Same Number, New Subject

H.C.R. 9 (2026)

Sponsor: Rep. Joseph Elison (primary) / Sen. Scott D. Sandall

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEIntroduced Jan. 30, 2026 as "Concurrent Resolution Addressing the National Debt." It "urges Congress to resolve the national debt" and to take "action towards balancing the federal budget."
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSThe 1st substitute, adopted in House committee on Feb. 26, 2026, replaced it with "Concurrent Resolution Addressing the Great Salt Lake," which "urges federal assistance in stabilizing and restoring the Great Salt Lake." Same number, same sponsors.

A concurrent resolution states the Legislature's position; it does not change the law or spend money. The record still matters, because anyone following H.C.R. 9 by its first title would not know it became a Great Salt Lake resolution (status page; introduced text; 1st substitute).

The House adopted a 2nd substitute on the floor on March 3 and passed it 70–1–4. On March 6 the Senate turned down a 3rd substitute drafted by Sen. Nate Blouin and passed the resolution 26–1–2.

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Jan. 30, 2026Introduced as "Concurrent Resolution Addressing the National Debt."
  • Feb. 26, 2026House Natural Resources Committee substitute, 9–0–5: now about the Great Salt Lake.
  • March 3, 2026House floor substitute; House passed 70–1–4.
  • March 6, 2026Senate rejected a further substitute, then passed it 26–1–2.
  • March 25, 2026Signed by the governor.
C
Power: A resolution does not change anyone's rights or money.
Transparency: The whole subject changed under one number, though the change was made in an open committee vote and the new title says what the resolution does.
CASE 15 OF 19

Renamed, and Expanded, Ten Minutes Before the House Vote

S.B. 254 (2026)

Sponsor: Sen. Ann Millner (primary) / Rep. David Shallenberger

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEIntroduced Feb. 5, 2026 as "Critical Minerals Amendments," with a council that can designate critical minerals zones and a Critical Minerals Development Account. The Senate passed its version 21–2–6 on Feb. 24.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSA 3rd substitute filed at 7:48 p.m. on March 4 renamed it "Extracted Natural Resources Amendments." Its summary says it "creates the State Reinvestment Restricted Account and diverts various streams of income related to severance taxes" into it. The House adopted that substitute at 10:36 a.m. on March 5 and passed the bill ten minutes later.

The Senate then refused to agree to the House version. A conference committee wrote a 4th substitute that evening, and both chambers passed it on March 6: House 70–2–3, Senate 20–1–8. So both chambers did vote on the final law. The Senate's first vote was on a narrower critical-minerals bill (status page; introduced text; 3rd substitute; enrolled text).

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Feb. 9, 2026Senate committee, 4–1–2.
  • Feb. 23–24, 2026Senate floor substitute; passed 28–1–0 and 21–2–6.
  • March 2, 2026House committee, 9–0–5.
  • March 4, 2026, 7:48 p.m.Renamed 3rd substitute filed: "Extracted Natural Resources Amendments."
  • March 5, 2026, 10:36 a.m.House adopts it; passes the bill 60–12–3 at 10:46 a.m.
  • March 5, 2026, 8:36 p.m.Conference committee substitute, 6–0–0.
  • March 6, 2026Final passage: House 70–2–3, Senate 20–1–8.
  • March 26, 2026Signed by the governor.
C
Power: A new restricted account and new severance-tax routing arrived in the second chamber.
Transparency: The expansion was filed the night before and adopted ten minutes before a House vote, but both chambers then voted on the final version.

Note for readers of our Bill-Tracker page on S.B. 254 (Oct. 18, 2026): That page says the changes "never went back through a floor vote." The status page shows final passage votes in both chambers on March 6, 2026, after the conference committee. That page will be corrected.

CASE 16 OF 19

The Water Limit That Lost Its Number

S.B. 225 (2026)

Sponsor: Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson (primary) / Rep. Tyler Clancy

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEIntroduced as "Utah Inland Port Authority Amendments." It barred using the Inland Port Authority's property tax differential to recruit new development in its Salt Lake City land that would use more than 200,000 gallons of drinking water a day.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSThe 1st substitute renamed it "Public Corporation Amendments." The 2nd substitute made the rule statewide and replaced the 200,000-gallon number with whatever limit the local water provider's own rules set.

Both changes in the water rule came in the 2nd substitute, adopted in the House Economic Development and Workforce Services Committee on Feb. 26, 2026 (9–0–1). The 3rd substitute, adopted on the House floor March 6, left that wording the same. The full version-by-version history is in our Bill-Tracker case file (status page).

The only person on record speaking in favor at the Senate committee was Charles Akerlow, lead developer on the Tooele Valley inland port project; seven people spoke against it, per that committee's minutes as summarized in our case file.

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Jan. 30, 2026Introduced as "Utah Inland Port Authority Amendments."
  • Feb. 5, 2026Senate committee 1st substitute, 3–0–3: renamed "Public Corporation Amendments."
  • Feb. 23, 2026Senate passed it 20–5–4.
  • Feb. 26, 2026House committee 2nd substitute, 9–0–1: statewide, no fixed gallon limit.
  • March 6, 2026House passed it 67–2–6 at 5:54 p.m., then substituted again and passed it 57–10–8 at 6:00 p.m.
  • March 18, 2026Signed by the governor.
D
Power: A fixed, statewide-checkable number became a limit set separately by each water provider.
Transparency: The title stopped naming the Inland Port Authority, and the minutes record no debate on the water change itself.
CASE 17 OF 19

A Bill That Died Inside a Bill That Passed

H.B. 477 (2026)S.B. 284 (2026)

H.B. 477: Rep. Jill Koford (primary) / Sen. Kirk A. Cullimore  ·  S.B. 284: Sen. Lincoln Fillmore (primary) / Rep. Jill Koford

WHAT IT LOOKED LIKEH.B. 477, "Land Use Regulation Revisions," covered city incorporation requests, planning commissions, infrastructure completion assurances and detached accessory dwelling units. It never got a committee vote and was closed out March 6.
WHAT THE RECORD SHOWSThe same provisions appear in S.B. 284, "Local Land Use Modifications," from its 1st substitute on. S.B. 284 grew from about 26,000 words to about 37,000, passed, and was renamed "Local Land and Water Modifications" on the session's last night.

S.B. 284's introduced text does not mention detached accessory dwelling units; its 1st substitute, filed Feb. 23, 2026, mentions them 24 times, matching H.B. 477. Its summary adds H.B. 477's items word for word, such as amending "requirements for a modified feasibility request related to a proposed municipal incorporation" (H.B. 477; S.B. 284 introduced; S.B. 284 1st substitute).

The Senate passed S.B. 284 on Feb. 26, 21–3–5. In the House, the bill was held until the last night: a 6th substitute was adopted at 9:09 p.m. on March 6, the House passed it at 9:16 p.m. (61–7–7), and the Senate agreed at 11:43 p.m. (24–3–2), sixteen minutes before the session ended (status page; H.B. 477 status page).

The timeline, from the bill's own status page
  • Feb. 10, 2026S.B. 284 introduced as "Local Land Use Modifications."
  • Feb. 23–24, 2026S.B. 284 1st substitute adds H.B. 477's provisions; Senate adopts it.
  • Feb. 26, 2026Senate passed S.B. 284 21–3–5.
  • March 2, 2026H.B. 477 returned to Rules by House committee, no vote recorded.
  • March 6, 2026, 9:09 p.m.House adopts 6th substitute, renamed "Local Land and Water Modifications"; passed 9:16 p.m.
  • March 6, 2026, 11:43 p.m.Senate concurred 24–3–2.
  • March 6, 2026, 11:59 p.m.H.B. 477 closed out.
  • March 18, 2026S.B. 284 signed by the governor.
D
Power: Land-use rules that never got their own hearing became law inside a different bill.
Transparency: Anyone following H.B. 477 would see it fail; the same text passed under another number and, at the end, another name.

Correction to our earlier notes (Oct. 18, 2026): Those notes said S.B. 284 was retitled in its 5th and 7th substitutes. The version texts show the original title was already "Local Land Use Modifications," and it became "Local Land and Water Modifications" in the 6th substitute, the version that became law.
